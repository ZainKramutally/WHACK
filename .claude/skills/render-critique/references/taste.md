# The taste lens: what an art director looks for

Read this before every critique. Each section gives the **principle**, **what great looks like**, the **red flags** to name out loud, and **benchmarks** to compare against. Use the vocabulary precisely. Critique with conviction, as a lead would at a review table: "I'd do X because Y".

---

## 1. Value: the skeleton (check this first)
If the value structure fails, colour can't save it.
- **Principle:**
  - A strong image reads in **2–4 value masses** (notan). Squint, and it should still be clear what it is and where to look.
  - Value carries form, depth and focus. Hue carries mood.
- **Great:**
  - One dominant value family (a mostly dark or mostly light image), plus a clear minority.
  - The **highest contrast sits at the focal point** only.
  - Value groups are simple and joined: lights connect to lights, darks to darks ("passages").
  - There's a deliberate key: a high key (airy), a low key (moody), or a full range used on purpose.
- **Red flags:**
  - "Everything mid-grey" (a flat value range).
  - "Spotty" (lights and darks scattered evenly, so no mass reads).
  - Contrast equal everywhere, so nothing leads.
  - The focal object has *less* contrast than its surroundings.
  - Background darks as dark as the foreground darks, which kills depth.
- **Benchmarks:**
  - Mike Mignola (big black shapes)
  - Edward Hopper (simple light masses)
  - Studio Ghibli backgrounds (Kazuo Oga), where depth comes from value steps
  - Richard Schmid, *Alla Prima*: "value does the work"

## 2. Colour: theory you actually apply
- **Hue harmony.** Name the scheme you see: analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic, or monochrome with an accent. Good schemes have **one dominant hue family (~60–80%), a secondary (~20–30%) and a small accent (~5–10%)** (60-30-10). The accent is the most saturated and usually the complement of the dominant.
- **Temperature.** Light and shadow should differ in temperature: warm light means cool shadows, or the reverse (Gurney, *Color and Light*). Same-temperature light and shadow reads as dead or plastic. Temperature can push depth too: cooler and lighter as things recede (aerial perspective), or into a coloured atmosphere.
- **Saturation hierarchy.** Saturation is a spotlight. Big areas sit muted, and the most saturated pixels belong to the focal or living element. Full saturation everywhere is "candy shop" and causes fatigue.
- **Itten's seven contrasts.** Name the one the image is built on, and whether it's used deliberately: hue, light–dark, cold–warm, complementary, simultaneous, saturation, extension (area proportion).
- **Simultaneous contrast (Albers).** A colour shifts depending on its neighbours. A grey beside orange reads blue. Use it, and watch for accidental muddy shifts.
- **Colour script.** Across scenes, each world needs a distinct colour identity, readable as a thumbnail strip (Pixar colour scripts, Mary Blair, Eyvind Earle).
- **Mud check.** Desaturated mid-value colours in the wrong place, browns and greys that weren't chosen, read as dirty. Darks should be rich (deep blue, violet, oxblood), not black-grey.
- **Glow.** Glow reads when it is (a) brighter *and* more saturated at the core edge than the field, (b) seated in a darker surround, and (c) tinting its neighbours a little (light spill). Glow on a light field looks like paint, not light.
- **Benchmarks:**
  - Moebius (flat poster colour, one fire)
  - Syd Mead (a light-temperature split)
  - *Journey*, *Sky* (thatgamecompany): a saturation hierarchy with a warm accent
  - *Firewatch* (Olly Moss): two-to-three-hue posters
  - *Subnautica*: one field per biome, an opposite small accent

## 3. Shape language: what forms say
- **Principle:**
  - Circles and curves read as safe, soft, organic or young.
  - Squares and blocks read as stable, heavy and old.
  - Triangles and spikes read as dynamic, dangerous, alien or energetic.
  - A world should **pick a dominant shape family** and use the others as contrast at the focal point.
- **Big–medium–small.**
  - Shapes need size variety: one big mass, a few medium, many small.
  - Equal-sized repeated shapes read as pattern or noise ("polka dots", "confetti").
  - Group the small things into clusters with gaps (rhythm), not even scatter.
- **Silhouette.**
  - Every important element should be recognisable as a filled black shape.
  - Look for distinctive negative-space notches.
  - Avoid "blob" silhouettes.
  - Overlap shapes to create depth, but keep **tangents** out: edges that just kiss create visual tension and flatten space.
- **Rhythm and repetition with variation.** Repeat a motif at different scales and spacings, so it feels like music, not wallpaper. The interval between elements matters as much as the elements.
- **Straights against curves.** Contrast is interest: a curved world needs a few straight accents, a straight world a few curves.
- **Benchmarks:**
  - Mignola, Mary Blair (bold simple shapes)
  - *Alto's Odyssey*, *Hyper Light Drifter* (strong silhouette worlds)
  - Moebius (organic forms against sharp spires)

## 4. Composition: where the eye goes
- **Focal hierarchy.**
  - One primary focal point, one or two secondary, everything else supports.
  - The focal point gets the highest value contrast, the most saturation, the sharpest edges, the most detail, and sits where lines lead.
  - When three things fight for attention, nothing wins.
- **Placement.** Use thirds or the golden section for the focal point, or a deliberate central symmetry for grandeur. Avoid "almost centred" (it reads as a mistake) and avoid putting the focal point dead centre of an asymmetric scene.
- **Leading lines and flow.** Paths, ridges, stalks and light shafts should point at the focal point. Check how the eye enters (usually bottom-left in Western reading), travels, and rests. Lines that run out of the frame drag the eye out.
- **Framing and depth layers.** A dark foreground frame, a lit middle ground and a soft background give the classic three-plane depth (repoussoir). Each plane should differ in value, saturation, detail and edge sharpness.
- **Visual weight and balance.** Big, dark, saturated or detailed elements are heavy. Asymmetric balance (a big mass against a small accent) is more alive than mirror symmetry. A heavy element at an edge pulls the frame over.
- **Negative space.** Empty areas are **rest**, and they make the busy areas sing. But there's a difference: *dead space* (unintentional, flat, no gradient, no purpose) versus *breathing space* (shaped, a gradient, it frames the subject). A third of the frame of flat floor is dead space.
- **Horizon and camera.** A low horizon gives sky drama or makes the viewer small and the world large. A high horizon gives intimacy with the ground. A tilted horizon brings unease (use it on purpose only).
- **Flip test.** A good composition still works mirrored. If the flip feels wrong, your eye was compensating for an imbalance.
- **Benchmarks:**
  - Andrew Loomis (*Creative Illustration*)
  - Ghibli layouts
  - Roger Deakins framing (one light idea per shot)

## 5. Edges and detail density
- **Edge control.**
  - Hard edges pull attention, soft edges recede, and lost edges merge forms (atmosphere, mystery).
  - Hard edges belong at the focal point, softness elsewhere.
  - All-hard edges read as "cut-out" or sticker-like; all-soft reads as mush.
  - In pixel art the equivalents are hard silhouettes for the focal point and stepped-down value contrast elsewhere.
- **Detail density.**
  - Detail is a budget.
  - Spend it at the focal point and on silhouettes.
  - Let large areas rest (texture at most, no new information).
  - Uniform detail density everywhere is the main cause of "messy".
- **Noise against texture.** Texture supports form and material. Noise (speckle, high-frequency patterns without meaning, aliasing) just adds fatigue.

## 6. Light logic
- One clear light idea per scene: a key direction or a dominant source (sun, glow, sky), with shadows consistent with it.
- Light-and-shadow pattern: big shapes of light across the scene (for example a lit path, a pool of glow) are stronger than evenly lit everything.
- Rim or back light separates silhouettes from the background where values are close.
- Bounce and fill take the colour of their source (the ground colour, the glow colour), never neutral grey.
- Emissive worlds: the glow sources *are* the key light. The terrain near them should catch their colour, or the glow looks pasted on.

## 7. Motion: taste in animation
Judge motion like an animator. Apply Disney's principles where they matter for environments:
- **Timing and spacing.** Slow, large things move slowly (giant flora sway over 4–8 s), and small things faster. Same-period motion everywhere looks mechanical and "seasick".
- **Ease in and out.** Nothing should move at constant speed or snap. Sway should have soft extremes.
- **Overlapping action and follow-through.** Tips lag bases (a stalk bends, then its cap follows). Fronds wave in a travelling wave, not in unison.
- **Offset / stagger.** Neighbouring instances must be out of phase. Synchronised breathing glow reads as a screensaver.
- **Secondary motion.** Small drifting particles, flicker on glow and shimmer on water add life without stealing focus. They should be lower contrast than the hero motion.
- **Arcs.** Organic motion travels on arcs, not straight lines.
- **Motion hierarchy.** The focal area has the most interesting motion, and the background is near-still. Motion is attention, so budget it like detail.
- **Calm-but-alive.** For idle environments, aim for a 6–12 s rhythm: a breath, a sway, a drift. Avoid dead air (nothing moves) and jitter (high-frequency noise, aliasing crawl, shadow swimming, popping).
- **Loopability** when it's for a trailer or GIF: no visible reset.
- **Benchmarks:**
  - *Ori* (secondary motion on environments)
  - *Sky* (cloth and flora follow-through)
  - Studio Ghibli wind passes through grass in waves
  - *Subnautica* kelp sway against the stillness of rock

## 8. Taste and judgement (the part that matters)
- **Intention over correctness.** Rules can be broken if the break is deliberate and serves the mood. Say whether something reads as *chosen* or *accidental*.
- **Restraint.** Most "not beautiful" images have too much: too many hues, too many focal points, too much detail, too much motion. The fix is usually *remove and group*, not *add*.
- **Specific over generic.** Never say "improve composition". Say "the twin caps at top-left are as bright as the crown, so the eye splits. Drop them one value step and the grove becomes the only lit mass."
- **Name the emotion.** What should the viewer feel? Does each choice serve it? (Awe needs scale and calm. Mystery needs lost edges and darkness. Wonder needs one surprising colour.)
- **Paintover thinking.** For each fix, describe the paintover you'd do: what you'd darken, simplify, group, recolour, move or delete, and what the image looks like after.
- **Compare with a master.** For every major note, name one reference that solves that problem well, and what to take from it.
