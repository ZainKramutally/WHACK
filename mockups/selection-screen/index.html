Appendix A: Why silicon, and why it is doubtful

  1. Silicon is everywhere, yet life chose carbon. Earth’s crust is about 28% silicon and 0.02% carbon, but living things are about 18% carbon.
  2. Silicon bonds to itself weakly: Si–Si is roughly 220 kJ/mol against roughly 350 for C–C, so its chains fall apart more easily. Cold slows that down.
  3. Silicon bonds to oxygen extremely strongly. Free oxygen or liquid water would lock it away as rock, so this world has neither.
  4. Silanes burn on contact with oxygen and break down in water, which reinforces step 3.
  5. So the solvent must be cold and unreactive: liquid nitrogen [1].
  6. In the cold, silicon’s weakness becomes its strength. At −183 °C carbon chemistry is all but frozen, while silicon’s more reactive bonds can keep reacting [2].
  7. The seas set the air. Nitrogen is liquid only between about −210 °C and −147 °C, and the seas hold the air at their vapour pressure: about 3.6 bar at −183 °C [4].

Most chemists doubt this: almost nothing dissolves in liquid nitrogen, and chemistry there is extremely slow. Petkowski, Bains and Seager [2] conclude that no environment makes silicon-based life plausible. This world is our best-reasoned guess, not a prediction.
